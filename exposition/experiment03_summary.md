
# Experiment 3 Summary

In Experiments 01 and 02, small synthetic examples showed how an edge between two nodes behaves when a third variable is a confounder or a collider. Telling the two apart is essential because they behave in opposite ways: failing to control for a confounder can make a factor with no effect look highly significant, while controlling for a collider biases the estimated effect, even flipping its sign, and at the same time raises the adjusted R², so the mistake looks like an improvement.[^1] In Experiment 03, eight widely used equity factors (the five Fama-French factors,[^2] momentum,[^3] and short-term[^4] and long-term reversal[^5]) are used to test for collider and confounder bias on real data, following the methodology of López de Prado and Zoonekynd's _Causality and Factor Investing: A Primer_.[^6] The daily factor returns, from July 1963 to August 2026, come from Kenneth French's Data Library.[^7] López de Prado and Zoonekynd ran the PC causal discovery algorithm[^8] on the factor returns of each of 85 Barra risk models, covering a diverse range of markets, and kept the edges that appeared in at least one third of the resulting graphs. I applied the same algorithm[^9] to the eight factors. Because PC rests on the strong assumption that no two variables in the dataset share an unmeasured common cause, I first drew my own causal graph, with four hypothesized hidden confounders, and wrote down predictions before running the algorithm. I then ran PC over six separate decades to see which edges were stable, as in the Primer, with one notable difference: they repeated the algorithm across markets, whereas I repeated it across time.

The results show the limits of PC on this data. In three of the seven runs, and in the aggregated graph itself, PC produced a cycle, which cannot exist in a valid causal graph. The edges themselves were fairly stable, with four pairs appearing in all six decades, but their directions were not: most pairs reversed direction between periods. The one-third rule also filtered very little, keeping 22 of the 28 possible pairs, since with this much data nearly every pair is significant in some period. Several of the arrows were also hard to believe economically, such as the market's return being caused by small long-short portfolios.

Replicating Exhibit 5 on the aggregated graph produced one sign flip among eight collider triples, starting from a beta close to zero, while adding the "collider" raised adjusted R² in seven of the eight cases. The exhibit could not be replicated under my own graph, because all of its colliders involve hidden variables. More importantly, the one flip depended on the market being an _effect_ of the other two factors. If the market is instead a common cause, the same regression corrects confounder bias rather than introducing collider bias. Which interpretation holds depends on the direction of the arrows, which is precisely what PC could not settle.

My own graph also failed in several places. Two of my predictions were wrong: short-term reversal was linked to both momentum and the market. I then tested two of my hidden confounders with observable proxies: changes in the 10-year Treasury yield for interest-rate surprises, and changes in the corporate credit spread and the VIX for liquidity.[^10] Neither set of proxies explained the links they were meant to explain. The most important finding was that these relationships change over time. The correlation between yield changes and the market reversed sign around 2000, consistent with the documented shift in the stock-bond correlation,[^11] and the VIX's correlation with the size factor reversed around 2008. In two cases, controlling for a variable revealed a link that the full sample had hidden. A single causal graph estimated over sixty years cannot represent arrows that change sign, whether it is drawn by a researcher or discovered by an algorithm.

These conclusions have limits. Real data has no known true graph, so these results concern robustness, not correctness. A weak proxy cannot show that a confounder is absent, only that the chosen measure does not explain the link. Two of my four hypothesized confounders could not be tested, and my graph was refined with the help of an AI assistant. Experiment 03 therefore shows that a theory-first graph and a data-driven graph disagree on real data, but not which one is right. Experiment 04 addresses that question with synthetic data generated from a known causal graph.
